## Key Points

- The article identifies the 'Matthew Effect' in RL for LLMs: RL improves performance on tasks in proportion to initial competence, making easy tasks easier while hard tasks often remain difficult.
- Analysis of math, code, and agentic RL shows gains from RL are proportional to problem difficulty, with hardest problems receiving minimal improvement.
- The problem isn't just lack of gradient signal for unsolved problems, but wasted compute on easy problems that get frequent rare incorrect solutions.
- The proposed solution 'Never Give Up' (NGU) adaptively adjusts sampling: starts with small k samples, but if all completions are wrong, continues sampling with probability p to give harder problems more chances.
- NGU reallocates compute from easy problems (quickly filtered) to harder problems, achieving better performance on hard problem subsets across domains.
- Experiments on GSM8k show NGU outperforms all fixed k values of standard GRPO, particularly on hardest problem subsets.
- At larger scales with math RL (DeepScaler on Qwen 3 4B), NGU further improves performance on hardest subsets of AIME + BRUMO 2025 evaluations.
- For code RL on Manufactoria, where tests vary in difficulty within problems, NGU helps overcome stagnation by pushing models to solve progressively harder tests.
- The Matthew Effect resembles primacy bias but is not caused by plasticity issues - LLMs can recover from early bad samples.
- Limitations include reduced effectiveness for tasks dominated by very difficult problems and potential for more off-policy early samples.
